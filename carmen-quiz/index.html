Part 2 — Translations: Easy

8. In the opening scene, the bored soldiers people-watch in the square and sing Drôles de gens que ces gens-là! What are they saying?

Drôle means funny or odd. Sur la place, chacun passe, chacun vient, chacun va: everyone comes and goes, and the soldiers have nothing better to do than make snarky comments about them.

9. The factory bell rings, the young men gather in the square and sing La cloche a sonné. What are they waiting for?

La cloche a sonné, nous venons ici guetter le retour: the bell has rung, and we’ve come to watch for you. They plan to follow the brunes cigarières (dark-haired cigarette girls), murmuring sweet nothings.

10. The young men (tenors, that’s you) crowd around Carmen and beg: Carmen! sois gentille, au moins réponds-nous, et dis-nous quel jour tu nous aimeras! What do they ask, and what’s her answer?

Quand je vous aimerai? Ma foi, je ne sais pas… Peut-être jamais, peut-être demain; mais pas aujourd’hui, c’est certain. Then she launches straight into the Habanera.

11. A fight breaks out inside the factory and the women’s chorus splits in two, one side shouting C’est la Carmencita! and the other Non, non! ce n’est pas elle! What are they arguing about?

Zuniga is trying to get a straight story and both halves of the chorus shout over each other, singing different words at the same time. Know which side you’re on!

12. Our Act 4 starts with Les voici! Voici la quadrille! and then Sur les lances le soleil brille, en l’air toques et sombreros! What’s going on?

La quadrille (Spanish cuadrilla) is a matador’s team, not the dance. Toques et sombreros are caps and hats, and up they go.

13. Leading the Act 4 procession is the alguazil, and the crowd greets him with À bas! à bas! Who is the alguazil, and why the boos?

Alguazil (Spanish alguacil) is a constable or bailiff, a petty officer of the law. The libretto calls him l’alguazil à vilaine face, the ugly-faced constable. Everyone who follows him gets a hero’s welcome; the policeman gets jeered. À bas = “down with,” not là-bas (“over there”). Boo him with gusto.

14. All through the procession you switch between voici (Voici, débouchant sur la place…, Voici les banderilleros!) and voyez (Voyez les banderilleros!, Voyez les picadors!). What’s the difference?

Voici comes from vois ici, “see here”: here is, here come. It announces an arrival (Les voici! Voici la quadrille!). Voyez is the command form of voir: “look!” It points out what’s already in front of you (Voyez quel air de crânerie!). Sing voici as they enter and voyez as you show them off. (Voilà = “there is.”)
Part 3 — Translations: Intermediate

15. Moralès and the soldiers try to get Micaëla to wait with them for Don José. She slips away, promising to come back quand la garde montante remplacera la garde descendante. When is that?

La garde montante is the guard coming on duty; la garde descendante is the guard going off. Don José is with the relief guard, so that’s when she’ll find him. She’s not falling for the soldiers’ “stay and wait with us” routine.

16. The cigarette girls drift out on their break, watching their smoke rise, and decide that lovers’ sweet talk, raptures and vows are all fumée. What’s the joke?

Le doux parler des amants, c’est fumée! Leurs transports et leurs serments, c’est fumée! They spend the whole chorus dreamily following the smoke (Dans l’air, nous suivons des yeux la fumée), then land the punchline: men’s promises are just as flimsy.

17. In the Toreador Song refrain, everyone sings Et songe bien, oui, songe en combattant, qu’un œil noir te regarde, et que l’amour t’attend. What is the œil noir?

“And think, while you’re fighting, that a dark eye is watching you, and that love awaits you.” On stage everyone knows whose dark eyes those are: Carmen’s. (The evil eye would be le mauvais œil.)

18. End of Act 2: Zuniga bursts in, José draws his sabre, and the smugglers disarm the officer. Then they ask him, very politely: Vous viendrez avec nous? Consentez-vous? Répondez, camarade! What’s going on?

“You’ll come with us? Do you consent? Answer, comrade!” Carmen calls it une promenade, a little stroll. They can’t let him go and report them, so they keep him for an hour. Zuniga replies that their argument (pistols) is one you can hardly resist, and warns them to watch out later. Le Dancaïre shrugs: La guerre, c’est la guerre, and everyone joins in: Passez devant sans vous faire prier (“After you, and don’t make us ask twice”).

19. At the end of Act 2 everyone sings about la chose enivrante. What is this intoxicating thing?

Le ciel ouvert, la vie errante, pour pays l’univers, et pour loi sa volonté, et surtout la chose enivrante: la liberté! Open sky, a wandering life, the whole world for a country, your own will for law — and above all, freedom. It’s the smugglers recruiting José, and the opera’s big idea in one chorus.

20. In the Act 3 smuggling ensemble, Carmen, Frasquita and Mercédès tell the gang Quant au douanier, c’est notre affaire! What’s their plan?

“As for the customs officer, that’s our business!” The women are completely confident their charm will get the smugglers through. The men are happy to let them try.

21. Micaëla tells José his mother is dying, and he finally agrees to leave. His parting words to Carmen, in front of the whole gang: Sois contente, je pars, mais nous nous reverrons. What does he say?

It’s a threat, not a goodbye. Moments earlier, when he swore he wouldn’t leave even if it cost him his life, everyone sang Ah! prends garde, prends garde, Don José! As he drags Micaëla off, Escamillo’s Toreador refrain drifts back from the distance.

22. The very last lines. As the crowd pours out of the arena, José tells them: Vous pouvez m’arrêter. C’est moi qui l’ai tuée. What does he say?

Arrêter means both “stop” and “arrest.” Then: Ah! Carmen! ma Carmen adorée! A moment ago you were inside the ring singing Victoire! and the Toreador refrain.
Part 4 — Translations: Advanced

23. In the fight chorus the women retell how it all started. Manuelita announced she’d buy herself a donkey, and Carmen, railleuse à son ordinaire, said: Un âne, pour quoi faire? Un balai te suffira. What’s the dig?

Railleuse à son ordinaire = mocking, as usual. Witches don’t need donkeys; they fly on broomsticks.

24. Manuelita fires back: Pour certaine promenade mon âne te servira, and the women add that two lackeys will follow behind, t’émouchant à tour de bras. What “little ride” is she wishing on Carmen?

Convicts were paraded through the streets on a donkey and whipped. Émoucher means to swat the flies off, so “two lackeys swatting the flies off you with all their might” is a sarcastic way of saying a flogging. Là-dessus toutes les deux se sont prises aux cheveux: at that, the two of them grabbed each other by the hair.

25. Act 1 finale. Just before she’s marched off to prison, Carmen whispers to José: En chemin je te pousserai aussi fort que je le pourrai… Laisse-toi renverser… le reste me regarde! What’s the plan the crowd is about to see play out?

Le reste me regarde = the rest is my business. At the bridge she shoves him, he lets himself go over, and she escapes, flinging her rope over the parapet, while the cigarette girls crowd round Zuniga avec de grands éclats de rire, roaring with laughter. That laughter is you.

26. Act 3 finale. Before leaving the smugglers’ camp, Escamillo announces Je vous invite tous aux courses de Séville… Et qui m’aime y viendra! — looking straight at Carmen. What is he saying?

Les courses here are bullfights (courses de taureaux), not races. A public invitation with a private target: José lunges at him and Le Dancaïre and Le Remendado have to hold him back while Escamillo strolls off, very slowly.

27. As the banderilleros march in, the crowd sings Voyez les banderilleros, voyez quel air de crânerie! What are you admiring?

Crânerie = swagger, bravado (crâner = to show off). It comes from crâne, skull, which is the trap. The crowd goes on to admire their flashing eyes and the glittering embroidery of their fighting costumes.

28. Escamillo arrives with Carmen on his arm and the crowd sings C’est l’Espada, la fine lame, celui qui vient terminer tout, qui paraît à la fin du drame et qui frappe le dernier coup. Why is this chilling, given what’s coming?

L’Espada is the swordsman, the matador who makes the kill; la fine lame = the fine blade. You’re cheering Escamillo, but the opera’s own last blow belongs to José, just outside the ring.

29. Act 4, after the procession. Four alguazils march on shouting Place! Place! Place au seigneur alcade! What are they shouting?

Place! here means “make room!”, not “square” or “seat.” The alcalde parades slowly across with his alguazils, and while everyone watches him, Frasquita and Mercédès slip over to warn Carmen.
Part 5 — What’s Happening Around You (Plot)

30. Why does Carmen get arrested in Act 1?

The factory girls come out, a fight breaks out inside, and witnesses report Carmen started it and used a knife. Zuniga orders Don José to arrest her.

31. What does Carmen do with a flower in Act 1 to set the whole story in motion?

Don José is deliberately not watching Carmen (he’s fiddling with the little chain for his priming pin, pointedly ignoring all the flirting going on). She walks right up and throws the flower at him. He keeps it.

32. How does Don José end up joining the smugglers between Acts 1 and 2?

Carmen seduces José into releasing her (the Seguidilla at the end of Act 1). He serves two months in jail. By Act 2 he’s just been released — but when Zuniga shows up at the tavern, José fights him and the smugglers have to take him in.

33. What do the card readings predict in Act 3?

Frasquita and Mercèdes draw happy cards. Carmen draws death, then draws again — still death. She accepts it with fatalistic calm. This is one of the most important emotional moments in the opera.

34. Why does Micaëla come to find Don José in Act 3?

Micaëla actually sneaks into the smugglers’ mountain hideout alone to find him. It takes courage. She does still love him, but the stated reason is his dying mother.

35. By Act 4, Carmen is in love with:

Carmen moved on from Don José during Act 3. She and Escamillo arrive at the bullfight together. She’s happy. This makes the ending even more brutal.

36. What is Lillas Pastia’s inn (the Act 2 setting)?

Think of it as a bar that’s also a front for smuggling operations. The authorities tolerate it; Lillas Pastia is connected. It’s where Act 2 opens.
Part 6 — The Big Picture

37. Don José hears a bugle call during Act 2 while Carmen is dancing for him. What happens?

The retreat bugle is the turning point of Act 2. Carmen sees it as proof that José values his uniform over her. She sings Au quartier! pour l’appel! Ah! j’étais vraiment trop bête! (“Back to barracks! For roll call! Ah, what a fool I was!”), calls him a canary (Va-t’en donc, canari), hurls his shako at him and tells him to take his sabre and go.

38. Carmen refuses to go back to Don José at the end of Act 4 even though she knows it may kill her. Why?

This is the opera’s central idea. Carmen told the cards she would die. She throws the ring José gave her at his feet anyway. Her freedom matters more than her life. This is why she’s a tragic hero, not just a victim.

39. How does the opera end?

The simultaneous triumph inside and murder outside is the finale’s central dramatic irony. The crowd’s cheers of Victoire! overlap with Carmen’s dying moments.

40. What happened to Don José between Acts 1 and 2 (why does Carmen look surprised when he walks into the tavern)?

José loosened the rope on Carmen’s wrists, she pushed him and ran. He was court-martialed, demoted, and served two months in military jail. He’s just been released when Act 2 begins. Carmen wasn’t sure he’d come.

41. Why does the Act 4 setting — the bullfighting arena — matter to the story?

The crowd celebrates inside while the private tragedy plays out just outside the gates. The drama depends on everything converging here: Escamillo winning, Carmen radiantly happy with him, and José who has lost everything.
